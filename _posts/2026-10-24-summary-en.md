### [Doctorow: Critiquing Others&\#x27; Work Beats LLM Feedback for Writers](https://pluralistic.net/2026/10/23/better-to-give/) ⭐️ 6.0/10

Cory Doctorow argues that the greatest benefit of peer writing workshops comes not from receiving feedback but from giving critique to others&\#x27; stories. He uses this to rebut the idea that LLM chatbots can replace workshops, saying chatbot editorial feedback cannot substitute for the skill development that comes from analyzing peers&\#x27; work.

rss · Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow · Oct 23, 20:44

**「Viewpoint」** Doctorow firmly believes that giving critique is more valuable than receiving it for developing writers, and that LLM editorial feedback is fundamentally inferior to peer workshops. He concedes that writers may find chatbot feedback useful \(and wouldn&\#x27;t stop them\), but insists it is a poor substitute for the creative instincts built by critiquing others.

**「Reasoning」** Doctorow&\#x27;s logic rests on the counterintuitive claim that workshop value is in the critiquing process, not the validation of receiving notes. He reasons that reading and re-reading others&\#x27; imperfect work to identify flaws and fixes develops unconscious creative instincts that improve one&\#x27;s own future stories. Since beginning writers produce mostly unsalvageable work, polishing it via LLM feedback yields less value than learning to avoid mistakes in the next story. He also notes that writers have little exposure to peers&\#x27; processes, making workshop analysis a unique form of critical training.

**「Concepts」** Peer workshop \(peer-based writing workshop\): a group of writers at similar career stages who read each other&\#x27;s work and offer feedback aimed at improvement. Doctorow describes its typical structure: manuscripts distributed and read in advance, structured spoken critiques, and rules against cross-talk or partial submissions. He frames it as a mechanism for building skill through active analysis rather than passive receipt of editorial notes.

### [Potter&\#x27;s Reading List: Housing Rules and Industrial Decline](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/reading-list-102426) ⭐️ 3.0/10

This is Brian Potter&\#x27;s weekly link roundup on construction, housing, and manufacturing, with one- or two-sentence annotations rather than a full essay. Items covered include minimum lot sizes, immigration enforcement&\#x27;s effect on homebuilding labor, Boeing&\#x27;s 737-10 certification, the US-China naval shipbuilding gap, and factory automation startups.

rss · Construction Physics · Oct 24, 12:01

**「Viewpoint」** Potter&\#x27;s scattered comments reflect his recurring concerns. He finds minimum lot sizes—rules requiring each home to sit on a parcel above a set area—to be a far more widespread barrier to small, cheap starter homes than he had realized, citing an AEI report that 90% of US jurisdictions have them. He treats signs of eroding US industrial capacity as alarming: domestic aluminum output is now 1% of the global total with no military-grade smelters, and China&\#x27;s combat-ship tonnage built over two years \(636,208 tons\) more than doubled America&\#x27;s \(270,509 tons\). He is mildly skeptical of blaming road design for rising pedestrian deaths, since it doesn&\#x27;t explain the sudden uptick around 2009.

**「Reasoning」** The roundup&\#x27;s implicit logic is that constraints on building—regulatory, labor, and industrial—explain persistent problems. Nearly two-thirds of homebuilders report immigration enforcement is squeezing their workforce, up from 23% in March, while modular and panelized construction remains stuck at 3% of single-family homes, suggesting factory methods still aren&\#x27;t relieving housing supply. On the industrial side, Potter links items—an optical fiber shortage driven by data centers, helium&\#x27;s re-listing as a critical material, and automation startups like Arda \($160 million raised\) that aim to make assembly lines reconfigurable—as evidence of strained physical capacity behind the AI boom.

**「Concepts」** A minimum lot size is a zoning rule requiring each house to occupy at least a specified area of land. By forbidding small parcels, it blocks compact, inexpensive starter homes even where buyers would want them, which is why the AEI report Potter cites treats it as a major supply restriction. Modular and panelized construction means fabricating whole rooms \(modular\) or flat wall and roof sections \(panelized\) in a factory for on-site assembly—an approach long proposed as a productivity fix for homebuilding, but one that has held only a 3% US market share for three straight years.
