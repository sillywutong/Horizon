### [Doctorow: Critiquing Others&\#x27; Work Beats LLM Feedback](https://pluralistic.net/2026/10/23/better-to-give/) ⭐️ 5.0/10

Cory Doctorow argues that the real value of a peer writing workshop lies in critiquing other writers&\#x27; stories, not in receiving feedback on your own. Because beginners who use chatbots only get the less valuable half of the exercise, he considers LLM editorial feedback a poor substitute for workshopping.

rss · Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow · Oct 23, 20:44

**「Viewpoint」** Doctorow believes beginning writers who treat LLMs as editors-on-demand misunderstand what improves writing. He concedes chatbot notes may have some value and says &quot;you do you&quot;—preferably with a local model rather than the big foundation models—but insists that getting editorial notes is &quot;distinctly inferior&quot; to offering criticism of others&\#x27; work, and that he would never ask a chatbot for such feedback himself.

**「Reasoning」** His logic runs in steps. First, workshops obviously help through external deadlines and peer validation, which prop up a beginner&\#x27;s fragile self-discipline. Second, counter-intuitively, receiving critiques is not the main benefit: closely analyzing someone else&\#x27;s imperfect draft and figuring out how to fix it builds the unconscious creative instincts that let you avoid pitfalls in your own future work, aided by the objectivity that comes from material you didn&\#x27;t write. Third, early work is mostly unsalvageable—the proverbial &quot;million words of crap&quot;—and careers are defined by a body of work, so learning to write the next story matters far more than patching one doomed draft. Fourth, the idea that an LLM can stand in for a workshop rests on the fallacy that a workshop&\#x27;s point is having people fix your story, rather than developing your own critical skills. He adds that writers, unlike musicians or painters, almost never observe each other&\#x27;s process, so each writer&\#x27;s method stays idiosyncratic—making self-built instincts essential. He closes with practical workshop rules: manuscripts read twice in advance, the author stays silent during critique, no cross-talk, no partial drafts, critique the story not the writer, and discourage resubmissions.

### [Construction Physics Reading List: Housing, Boeing, Manufacturing](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/reading-list-102426) ⭐️ 4.0/10

Brian Potter&\#x27;s weekly roundup curates links on construction and industrial technology, this week covering housing regulation, construction labor, Boeing&\#x27;s 737 MAX, US-China shipbuilding gaps, optical fiber shortages, and manufacturing startups. He adds brief commentary highlighting statistics he finds surprising or noteworthy, such as the near-universal prevalence of minimum lot sizes.

rss · Construction Physics · Oct 24, 12:01

**「Viewpoint」** Rather than advancing a single thesis, Potter signals ongoing concerns through his selections and asides: that land-use regulations like minimum lot sizes are a major barrier to affordable starter homes, that US industrial capacity is eroding in strategically sensitive areas such as aluminum smelting and naval shipbuilding relative to China, and that data-center demand is straining supply chains for inputs like optical fiber and power equipment. He also expresses mild skepticism of the WSJ attributing rising pedestrian deaths to road design, noting it cannot explain the timing of the increase around 2009.

**「Reasoning」** Potter grounds his commentary in cited data rather than extended argument. On housing, he cites an AEI report claiming 90% of US jurisdictions impose minimum lot sizes, and an NAHB finding that modular and panelized construction holds only 3% of the single-family market, reinforcing his longstanding interest in why housing supply and construction productivity stagnate. On labor, he notes a survey in which nearly two-thirds of homebuilders say immigration enforcement is affecting their workforce, up from 13% a year earlier. On industrial capacity, he quotes figures showing China built over twice the US tonnage of combat ships in the two years through December 2025, and that US aluminum output has fallen to 1% of the global total with no domestic smelter able to make military-grade high-purity aluminum. On technology, he notes a forecast that optical fiber spot prices could reach 2.8-5 times 2025 levels, and highlights startups like Arda, whose founder argues AI software could cut the months-long reconfiguration time for automated assembly lines.

**「Concepts」** Minimum lot sizes are zoning rules requiring each house to sit on a parcel above a set area; by banning small lots, they effectively outlaw cheaper starter homes. High-purity aluminum is a specialized grade needed for military applications, and the cited trade group warns the US and its NATO allies have no operating smelters capable of producing it. Long-term supply agreements \(LTAs\) are contracts locking in future purchases, which telecom operators are adopting as fiber supply tightens.
